# Source authors

The seven ancient authors that power the source-lens system. Each author is a lens: the galaxy re-shapes genealogies and stories according to what that author actually wrote. Author `id` values below are the canonical `SourceId` codes used in `data/sources.json` and in every `sources` array.

## Homer

- **id**: `homer`
- **Works**: Iliad, Odyssey
- **Period**: c. 750–700 BC
- **Language**: Ancient Greek (Homeric/Epic dialect)
- **Why included**: The oldest surviving Greek literary voice and the baseline against which every later mythographer positions himself; his portrayal of gods and heroes was treated as quasi-canonical throughout antiquity.
- **Coverage**: Trojan cycle (Iliad: the war; Odyssey: the returns), Olympian divine society and politics, heroic ethos, the underworld (Odyssey Book 11 Nekyia), early hero genealogies in passing. Almost no cosmogony, no systematic divine genealogy, and famously silent on later-canonical episodes (Trojan Horse only retrospectively, no Achilles' heel).

Known variant positions:

- Aphrodite is the daughter of Zeus and Dione (Iliad 5.370), flatly contradicting Hesiod's birth from sea-foam around Uranus' severed genitals.
- Oceanus and Tethys are called the origin of the gods (Iliad 14.201), a cosmogony incompatible with Hesiod's Chaos-first scheme.
- Oedipus continues to rule Thebes after the incest is revealed and dies there receiving funeral games (Odyssey 11.271–280; Iliad 23.679), against the blinded exile of Athenian tragedy.
- Achilles is woundable (grazed by Asteropaeus, Iliad 21.166–167) and has no invulnerable heel; that detail enters the tradition only in later sources such as Statius.

## Hesiod

- **id**: `hesiod`
- **Works**: Theogony, Works and Days
- **Period**: c. 700 BC
- **Language**: Ancient Greek (Epic dialect)
- **Why included**: The foundational systematic account of how the cosmos and the gods came to be; the Theogony is the reference genealogy that nearly every later divine family tree either copies or quietly corrects.
- **Coverage**: Cosmogony (Chaos, Gaia, the succession myth Uranus–Cronus–Zeus), divine genealogies, Titanomachy and Typhoeus, Prometheus and Pandora, the Five Ages of Man, Hecate, the Muses, catalogue of Zeus' wives. Thin on the Trojan cycle and individual hero sagas.

Known variant positions:

- Aphrodite is born from the sea-foam around Uranus' severed genitals (Theogony 188–200), versus Homer's daughter of Zeus and Dione.
- Pandora as the first woman and source of human ills (Theogony 570–612; Works and Days 60–105) is essentially Hesiod's own elaboration, absent from Homer.
- His Five Ages of Man (Works and Days 106–201) inserts an Age of Heroes into the metallic decline scheme, a structure no other early source shares.
- Works and Days 11–26 openly corrects the Theogony itself, declaring there are two Erides (Strifes), not one — an ancient author revising his own "facts".

## Pseudo-Apollodorus

- **id**: `apollodorus`
- **Works**: Bibliotheca (The Library), Epitome
- **Period**: 1st–2nd century AD (falsely attributed to Apollodorus of Athens, 2nd century BC)
- **Language**: Ancient Greek (Koine)
- **Why included**: The only surviving continuous summary of Greek myth from cosmogony to the death of Odysseus, and the closest thing antiquity left to a "reference database"; he frequently names his sources and records alternative versions, making him the natural spine for a variant-tracking system.
- **Coverage**: Nearly everything: cosmogony and divine genealogies (compressed), Gigantomachy (fullest surviving account), all major hero sagas (Perseus, Heracles with the canonical Twelve Labors, Theseus, the Argonauts, the Theban cycle), and via the Epitome the full Trojan cycle including events before and after the Iliad. Weak on cult, geography, and Roman material.

Known variant positions:

- Gives the canonical numbering logic of Heracles' Twelve Labors: Eurystheus refused to count the Hydra (Iolaus helped) and the Augean stables (payment demanded), so ten tasks became twelve (Bibliotheca 2.5) — a schema not found in earlier poetry.
- Records Helen as daughter of Zeus and Nemesis, hatched from an egg merely brought to Leda (Bibliotheca 3.10.7), alongside the familiar Leda version.
- Has Medea murder her brother Apsyrtus as a child aboard the Argo, dismembering him and scattering the limbs (Bibliotheca 1.9.24, following Pherecydes), directly contradicting Apollonius' adult Apsyrtus ambushed on land.
- His Epitome preserves the Judgment of Paris and the Wooden Horse in full narrative form — episodes Homer barely mentions — drawn from the lost Epic Cycle.

## Apollonius of Rhodes

- **id**: `apollonius`
- **Works**: Argonautica
- **Period**: 3rd century BC (composed c. 270–245 BC)
- **Language**: Ancient Greek (Hellenistic literary epic)
- **Why included**: The only complete surviving epic of the Argonaut saga and the definitive shape of the Jason–Medea story before Euripides' aftermath; a learned Hellenistic poet who consciously selects among older variants and embeds local aetiologies along the voyage route.
- **Coverage**: The Argonaut expedition end to end: catalogue of heroes, Lemnos, Hylas and Heracles, Phineus and the Harpies, the Clashing Rocks, Colchis and the Fleece, Medea's psychology (the first great interior love narrative), the return route geography, Talos in Crete. Little outside this saga.

Known variant positions:

- Apsyrtus is an adult commander pursuing the Argonauts and is ambushed and killed by Jason at a temple of Artemis with Medea's connivance (Argonautica 4.452–481), a deliberate softening of the older child-dismemberment version (Pherecydes, later Apollodorus and Ovid).
- Heracles is left behind in Mysia searching for Hylas (Argonautica 1.1207–1357) and never reaches Colchis, against traditions in which he completed the voyage or (per Herodorus) never sailed at all.
- Routes the return voyage up the Danube and down the Eridanus and Rhone into the western Mediterranean (Book 4), against Pindar's route home via Ocean, the Red Sea, and overland through Libya (Pythian 4).

## Ovid

- **id**: `ovid`
- **Works**: Metamorphoses
- **Period**: 43 BC – AD 17/18 (Metamorphoses completed c. AD 8)
- **Language**: Latin
- **Why included**: The single most influential mythological text in the Western tradition and the master source for transformation tales; a Roman poet who freely reshapes, psychologizes, and sometimes invents Greek material, making him the prime generator of "facts" later readers mistake for the Greek originals.
- **Coverage**: Transformations above all (c. 250 tales), a continuous mythic history from cosmogony to Julius Caesar's apotheosis, divine loves and punishments, Perseus, the Calydonian Hunt, a compressed Trojan section, and Roman reception (Aeneas, Romulus). Weak on cult practice and systematic genealogy.

Known variant positions:

- Medusa was a beautiful maiden transformed into a monster by Minerva after Neptune violated her in the goddess's temple (Metamorphoses 4.790–803), versus Hesiod, where the Gorgons are simply born monstrous sea-deities.
- Arachne's weaving contest with Minerva (Metamorphoses 6.1–145) has no surviving earlier source and may be largely Ovid's invention.
- Locates Proserpina's abduction at Enna in Sicily (Metamorphoses 5.385–408), against the Nysian plain of the Homeric Hymn to Demeter.
- Makes Daphne the daughter of the Thessalian river Peneus (Metamorphoses 1.452–567), while Pausanias and others make her daughter of the Arcadian river Ladon with a different surrounding story.

## Hyginus

- **id**: `hyginus`
- **Works**: Fabulae, Astronomica (De Astronomia)
- **Period**: 2nd century AD (transmitted under the name of Gaius Julius Hyginus, c. 64 BC – AD 17; modern scholarship treats the author as "Pseudo-Hyginus")
- **Language**: Latin
- **Why included**: A blunt but priceless Latin handbook: the Fabulae's plot summaries and name-lists preserve dozens of versions from lost Greek tragedies found nowhere else, and the Astronomica is the fullest surviving collection of catasterism (star-myth) narratives.
- **Coverage**: Encyclopedic plot summaries of nearly every saga, genealogical lists, catalogues (Argonauts, Helen's suitors, kings, "who killed whom"), plots of lost plays by Euripides and others, and star myths/constellation origins. The text is heavily abridged and frequently garbles names.

Known variant positions:

- Antigone survives: Haemon hides her among shepherds and she bears him a son, Maeon, who is later recognized at Thebes (Fabulae 72) — preserving Euripides' lost Antigone against Sophocles' double suicide.
- Odysseus feigns madness by yoking a horse and an ox together and sowing salt (Fabulae 95), a vivid detail absent from the older Cypria/Apollodorus version of Palamedes' test.
- His Argonaut catalogue (Fabulae 14) conflicts with Apollonius' roster in multiple names and parentages.
- The Astronomica routinely stacks rival catasterism identifications (e.g., Virgo as Erigone, Tyche, or Astraea/Dike, Astronomica 2.25) that disagree with Eratosthenes' and Aratus' attributions.

## Pausanias

- **id**: `pausanias`
- **Works**: Description of Greece (Periegesis Hellados, 10 books)
- **Period**: c. AD 150–175
- **Language**: Ancient Greek (Koine)
- **Why included**: A traveling eyewitness who records what each Greek region actually claimed about its own myths — sanctuary by sanctuary — making him the unrivaled source for local cult variants that contradict the Panhellenic literary versions.
- **Coverage**: Local cult variants and regional genealogies (especially Arcadia, Boeotia, Laconia, Corinthia), hero cults and tombs, foundation legends, descriptions of lost mythological art (Chest of Cypselus, paintings of Polygnotus), and rationalizing or skeptical asides on famous tales. Not a narrative source for the big sagas.

Known variant positions:

- Reports the Arcadian claim that Rhea saved the infant Poseidon by hiding him among lambs and giving Cronus a foal to swallow instead (8.8.2) — in Hesiod, Poseidon is simply swallowed and only Zeus is replaced (by a stone).
- Records pre-Euripidean Corinthian traditions about Medea's children: citing Eumelus, she buried them in Hera's sanctuary trying to make them immortal, and the Corinthians paid them cult after an oracle (2.3.6–11) — against Euripides' deliberate infanticide.
- Preserves the Phigalian cult of Black Demeter: a horse-headed Demeter who, raped by Poseidon in horse form, bore Despoina and the horse Arion (8.25.4–7, 8.42) — a startlingly archaic variant unknown to Panhellenic literature.
- Tells the Daphne story with her as daughter of the Arcadian river Ladon and adds the suitor Leucippus, who infiltrated her companions disguised as a girl (8.20.2–4), diverging sharply from Ovid's Peneus/Apollo-only version.

## Future lens candidates

Candidates recommended by the source review for later milestones, one line each:

- **Proclus' Epic Cycle summaries** — most pre- and post-Iliad Trojan material (Judgment of Paris, Achilles' heel, Wooden Horse, Iphigenia) reaches the current set only secondhand via Apollodorus' Epitome and Hyginus; a Proclus lens would show where "Homer" actually ends.
- **Homeric Hymns (c. 7th–5th century BC)** — the primary archaic narratives for Demeter/Persephone, Hermes' cattle theft, Apollo's birth, and Dionysus and the pirates, which the current seven only report secondhand.
- **Pindar (c. 518–438 BC)** — victory odes that openly rewrite myths on moral grounds (e.g. rejecting the cannibal version of Pelops' ivory shoulder in Olympian 1), ideal for showing deliberate ancient variant-editing.
- **Euripides (c. 480–406 BC)** — inventor or popularizer of many now-default versions (Medea killing her own children) plus radical variants (Helen never went to Troy), and the unnamed source behind many Hyginus entries.
- **Diodorus Siculus, Library Book 4 (1st century BC)** — a parallel systematic mythography with rationalizing/euhemerist tendencies, perfect as a contrast lens against Apollodorus on Heracles and the Argonauts.
- **Nonnus, Dionysiaca (5th century AD)** — the vast late-antique Dionysus epic preserving Dionysian and Typhon material absent from all seven current sources.
- **Eratosthenes' Catasterismi (epitome)** — the Greek counterpart to Hyginus' Astronomica, needed to corroborate or contradict star-myth claims that currently rest on a single source.

### Modeling caveats from the source review

- Pseudo-Apollodorus and Hyginus are imperial-era compilations that already aggregate older variants; when they write "but some say...", model the embedded attribution (e.g. "Apollodorus citing Pherecydes") rather than treating the compiler as a single voice.
- There is a ~450-year gap between Hesiod and Apollonius, so the archaic-lyric and classical-tragic stratum where many canonical variants were created is invisible in this set.
- Ovid and Hyginus refract Greek myth through Roman and Augustan sensibilities; surface language/era metadata in the UI so users do not mistake Ovidian inventions for Greek originals.
- The transmitted text of Hyginus' Fabulae is notoriously corrupt and abridged; consider a per-fact confidence flag for claims sourced only from Hyginus.
- Display "Pseudo-Apollodorus" and the Pseudo-Hyginus question honestly in lens metadata, since both works circulate under false ascriptions.
