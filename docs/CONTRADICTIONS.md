# Inter-author contradiction catalog

These contradictions power the source-lens dispute badges: under the `consensus` lens, a fact contested between authors is marked with a badge, and switching lenses re-wires the galaxy to that author's version. Every entry below was adversarially verified against the primary texts (Perseus Digital Library, ToposText) — claims and citations were checked line by line, and where the draft misstated a position, the corrected wording is what appears here. The kebab-case id under each heading is the `topic` key to use in data files.

## Aphrodite's birth

- **topic**: `aphrodite-parentage`
- **Characters**: `aphrodite`, `zeus`, `uranus`, `cronus`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod | Aphrodite grew from the sea-foam that gathered around Uranus' severed genitals after Cronus castrated him, coming ashore at Cythera and Cyprus. | Theogony 188–206 |
| Homer | Aphrodite is the daughter of Zeus and the goddess Dione, into whose lap she falls for comfort after Diomedes wounds her at Troy. | Iliad 5.370–417 |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | Aphrodite is listed among Zeus's children, borne to him by Dione. | Bibliotheca 1.3.1 |

Significance: the goddess of love has two utterly incompatible origins — cosmic castration versus ordinary divine parentage — a clash so famous that later Greeks invented two separate Aphrodites (Ourania and Pandemos) to resolve it.

Verification: ✓ verified — all three positions exact against Perseus; Frazer's own footnote on Bibliotheca 1.3.1 flags the conflict with Hesiod.

## Hephaestus' conception

- **topic**: `hephaestus-conception`
- **Characters**: `hephaestus`, `hera`, `zeus`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod | Hera bore Hephaestus entirely on her own, without union with any father, in angry rivalry with Zeus after he produced Athena from his head. | Theogony 927–929 |
| Homer | Hephaestus is the son of both Zeus and Hera, speaking of Zeus as his father and built the bedchamber Hera calls the work of "your own son." | Iliad 1.577–579, 14.338–339; Odyssey 8.312 |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | Hera gave birth to Hephaestus without intercourse, though Apollodorus explicitly notes that Homer instead makes him a child of Zeus and Hera. | Bibliotheca 1.3.5 |

Significance: whether Hephaestus has a father at all changes the whole Olympian family drama — in Hesiod he is Hera's solo retaliation against Zeus, and Apollodorus himself flags the disagreement with Homer.

Verification: ✓ verified — Apollodorus' wording confirmed nearly verbatim; minor nuance that Hesiod implies rather than states the Athena-birth motive (standard inference from the juxtaposition at Theogony 924–929).

## Hephaestus' fall from Olympus

- **topic**: `hephaestus-fall-from-olympus`
- **Characters**: `hephaestus`, `zeus`, `hera`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Homer | Zeus seized Hephaestus by the foot and hurled him from Olympus for taking Hera's side, and he fell all day before landing on Lemnos. | Iliad 1.590–594 |
| Homer | It was Hera who flung Hephaestus from heaven at birth, ashamed of his lameness, and Thetis and Eurynome rescued him in the sea. | Iliad 18.394–399 |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | Zeus cast Hephaestus out of heaven for coming to the rescue of Hera in her bonds, and the crash-landing on Lemnos is what lamed his legs. | Bibliotheca 1.3.5 |

Significance: Homer contradicts himself within the Iliad — two different throwers, two different landings — and the versions even disagree on whether the fall caused Hephaestus' lameness or his lameness caused the fall.

Verification: ✓ verified — both Iliad passages exact; caveat that "at birth" in the Iliad 18 version is standard scholarly inference (Homer says only that Hera wished to hide him for his lameness).

## What came first

- **topic**: `first-beings-cosmogony`
- **Characters**: `chaos`, `gaia`, `tartarus`, `eros`, `nyx`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod | First of all Chaos came to be, with nothing before it, followed by broad-breasted Gaia, dim Tartarus, and Eros. | Theogony 116–122 |
| Ovid | Chaos was not a being but a single shapeless disordered mass, which a god "and kinder nature" separated into sky, sea, and land. | Metamorphoses 1.5–32 |
| Hyginus | Chaos itself has a parent: it was born from Caligo (Mist), and from Chaos and Caligo came Night, Day, Erebus, and Aether. | Fabulae, preface (Theogony) 1–2 |

Verification: ✓ verified — Hyginus' "Ex Caligine Chaos" confirmed on ToposText; Ovid's "rudis indigestaque moles" and "deus et melior natura" confirmed in the Latin.

## Parents of the Fates (Moirai)

- **topic**: `moirai-parentage`
- **Characters**: `nyx`, `zeus`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod | Nyx bore the Moirai — Clotho, Lachesis, and Atropos — fatherless, among her dark brood of Doom, Death, and Nemesis. | Theogony 211–225 |
| Hesiod | The Moirai are daughters of Zeus and Themis, sisters of the Horae, "to whom wise Zeus gave the greatest honor." | Theogony 901–906 |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | By Themis, Zeus fathered the Fates: Clotho, Lachesis, and Atropus. | Bibliotheca 1.3.1 |
| Hyginus | The Parcae — Clotho, Lachesis, and Atropos — were born from Nox (Night) and Erebus. | Fabulae, preface (Theogony) 1 |

Significance: Hesiod gives the Fates two different sets of parents within the same poem — a contradiction with stakes, since Night-born Fates stand outside Zeus's order while Zeus-fathered Fates are part of his regime.

Verification: ✓ verified — all four positions confirmed, including the "Atropus" spelling in Frazer; some editors suspect Theogony 217–222 of interpolation, a textual-criticism nuance that does not affect the claim.

## Atlas and the sky

- **Characters**: `atlas`, `zeus`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod | Atlas, under hard constraint imposed by Zeus, stands at the western edge of the earth before the Hesperides, holding the broad sky on his head and tireless arms. | Theogony 517–520 |

## Did Prometheus create mankind?

- **topic**: `prometheus-creation-of-man`
- **Characters**: `prometheus`, `zeus`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod | Prometheus is mankind's benefactor and Zeus's deceiver at the division at Mecone, but humans already exist and are never said to be his creation. | Theogony 535–616; Works and Days 42–105 |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | Prometheus moulded men out of water and earth, and also gave them fire hidden in a fennel stalk without Zeus's knowledge. | Bibliotheca 1.7.1 |
| Ovid | The son of Iapetus shaped man from new-made earth mixed with rainwater, moulding him in the likeness of the all-governing gods. | Metamorphoses 1.76–88 |
| Hyginus | Prometheus, son of Iapetus, first fashioned men from clay ("Prometheus Iapeti filius primus homines ex luto finxit"). | Fabulae 142 |

Significance: Prometheus-as-sculptor of humanity, now the standard image, is entirely absent from Hesiod, our earliest source — a striking case of a "core" myth that accreted centuries later.

Verification: ✓ verified — Apollodorus and Hyginus confirmed verbatim; nuance that Ovid presents the Prometheus version as the second of two alternatives ("sive... sive..."), which does not affect the contradiction.

## Dionysus' first mother

- **topic**: `dionysus-first-birth`
- **Characters**: `dionysus`, `zeus`, `persephone`, `hera`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod | Semele, daughter of Cadmus, bore Zeus a splendid son, Dionysus — a mortal mother bearing an immortal child. | Theogony 940–942 |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | Dionysus is the son of Zeus and Semele; when Semele was incinerated by Zeus's lightning through Hera's trick, Zeus sewed the six-month child into his thigh and bore him at term. | Bibliotheca 3.4.3 |
| Hyginus | Liber was originally the son of Jove and Proserpina and was dismembered by the Titans; Jove gave his pounded heart to Semele in a drink, from which the god was conceived again — hence his epithet "the one with two mothers." | Fabulae 167 |

Significance: Hyginus preserves the Orphic "Zagreus" tradition in which Persephone, not Semele, is Dionysus' original mother — turning the familiar thigh-birth story into a rebirth.

Verification: ✓ verified — Fabulae 167 matches every element including the two-mothers epithet; note that Hyginus places the thigh-sewing at Fabulae 179, not 167, but the position does not attribute that detail to him.

## Persephone's split year

- **topic**: `persephone-split-year`
- **Characters**: `persephone`, `demeter`, `hades`, `zeus`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | Because Pluto gave her a pomegranate seed to eat, Persephone is compelled to remain a third of every year with him and the rest of the time with the gods. | Bibliotheca 1.5.3 |
| Ovid | Jupiter divides the revolving year into two equal parts (ex aequo), so Proserpina spends as many months with her mother as with her husband. | Metamorphoses 5.564–567 |
| Hyginus | Ceres obtained from Jove that Proserpina spend half the year with her and half with Pluto. | Fabulae 146 |

Verification: ✓ verified, Ovid position tightened — claim re-worded to the Latin's "ex aequo... totidem menses" phrasing and the citation narrowed from 5.564–571 to 5.564–567, where the operative lines sit; Frazer's note on Apollodorus 1.5.3 flags this ancient disagreement.

## Where Persephone was taken and who told Demeter

- **topic**: `persephone-abduction-details`
- **Characters**: `persephone`, `demeter`, `hades`, `zeus`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Ovid | Dis seized Proserpina as she gathered flowers by Lake Pergus near Henna in Sicily, and it was the nymph Arethusa who revealed to Ceres where her daughter had gone. | Metamorphoses 5.385–424, 5.487–508 |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | Pluto carried off Persephone secretly with Zeus's connivance, and Demeter learned the truth from the people of Hermione before quitting heaven in anger. | Bibliotheca 1.5.1 |
| Pausanias | Greek localities anchored the myth to their own soil with competing descent-points rather than a Sicilian meadow: the Eleusinians showed Erineus beside their Cephisus as the spot where Pluto descended to the underworld after carrying off the Maid, while the Argives pointed to a circuit of stones by the river Cheimarrus near Lerna as the place of his descent with Kore. | Description of Greece 1.38.5, 2.36.7 |

Significance: cities across the Mediterranean competed to claim the abduction for their own soil, so the "same" story comes anchored to entirely different landscapes and informants.

Verification: corrected — the original draft misattributed an Attic seizure-site claim to Pausanias 1.38.5; that passage records only a descent-point at Erineus (and the Argive rival descent-point is at 2.36.7). Pausanias nowhere places the seizure itself in Attica. The Ovid and Apollodorus positions verified unchanged.

## Athena's parentage

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod | Zeus swallowed his pregnant first wife Metis on the advice of Gaia and Ouranos (Earth and starry Heaven) and later himself gave birth to bright-eyed Athena (Tritogeneia) from his own head. | Theogony 886–900, 924–926 |

- **topic**: `ariadne-fate`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Homer | Artemis killed Ariadne on the island of Dia, on the testimony of Dionysus, before Theseus could bring her to Athens. | Odyssey 11.321–325 |
| Hesiod | Golden-haired Dionysus made Ariadne his wife, and Zeus made her immortal and unageing for his sake. | Theogony 947–949 |

## Who turned Callisto into a bear?

- **topic**: `callisto-transformation`
- **Characters**: `zeus`, `hera`, `artemis`

| Author | Claim | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Ovid | Juno, enraged after the birth of Arcas, seized Callisto by the hair and turned her into a bear herself, and Jupiter later set mother and son among the stars. | Metamorphoses 2.466–507 |
| Pseudo-Apollodorus | Zeus himself turned Callisto into a bear to hide the affair from Hera, who then persuaded Artemis to shoot her as a wild beast — though others say Artemis shot her for failing to keep her maidenhood. | Bibliotheca 3.8.2 |
| Hyginus | Juno in anger changed Callisto into a bear because she had lain with Jove, and Hyginus' star-myth handbook collects still more variants, including Diana killing the bear while hunting. | Fabulae 177; Astronomica 2.1 |

Verification: ✓ verified — Astronomica 2.1 confirmed in the Latin to stack the additional variants (Diana as transformer per Hesiod's version; Juno transforming with Diana killing) exactly as claimed.
